AHOY as a public benefit.

A white paper on converting AHOY’s independent media work into a community-serving nonprofit that helps local creators make, distribute, and keep ownership of their work.

Version 1.0August 2026Prepared for civic, philanthropic, and community partners

Executive summary

New Haven has extraordinary creative talent, but talent alone does not create durable local media. Artists need affordable production, trustworthy distribution, a place to gather, and a way to build an audience without surrendering their work to an opaque platform.

AHOY already points toward that missing civic layer: a New Haven-rooted independent media platform for music, podcasts, video, film, live events, and community updates. The proposal is to establish a Connecticut nonprofit successor—ideally a public charity recognized under Section 501(c)(3)—with a clear public mission and measurable programs. The nonprofit would preserve AHOY’s creator-first identity while making its core public-serving work eligible for charitable gifts, foundation support, government arts funding, and mission-aligned partnerships.

This is not a request to subsidize a private company. It is a proposal to separate a public-benefit mission from any commercial activity, put the mission under independent community governance, and make the results accountable to New Haven residents and creators.

Recommendation

Form a new Connecticut nonstock nonprofit corporation as AHOY’s public-benefit successor; seek 501(c)(3) recognition; use a qualified fiscal sponsor during the transition if funding is needed before approval; and keep any genuinely commercial activity in a separately governed, arm’s-length entity until nonprofit counsel approves a compliant structure and any asset transfer.

1. Proposed mission and public purpose

Mission: AHOY helps independent artists, musicians, filmmakers, podcasters, and community storytellers in Greater New Haven create, share, and sustain locally made media.

Vision: A New Haven where creative work can remain local, discoverable, fairly presented, and accessible to the public—regardless of an artist’s income, network, or existing platform reach.

The charitable case should rest on education, arts and culture, community development, and access to media—not on the promotion of one owner or one brand. Programs must be open through published criteria, serve a recognizable community, and document who benefits. The organization should prioritize New Haven residents and creators while welcoming regional participation when it advances the local mission.

Public benefit

Free or low-cost access to local culture, public events, educational resources, and a durable archive of New Haven creative work.

Creator benefit

Production support, practical training, audience development, and transparent distribution without requiring creators to give up ownership.

Civic benefit

More ways for residents, neighborhoods, institutions, and public leaders to encounter one another through local stories and culture.

Economic benefit

Paid opportunities, skills, visibility, and partnerships that strengthen the city’s independent creative economy.

2. A grant-ready program portfolio

Each program should have a budget, eligibility rules, an annual target, and a simple outcome report. The existing AHOY lanes—digital media, live community events, local creator discovery, and a future concert-studio model—can become a coherent public portfolio:

AHOY Open Archive

A free, searchable home for local music, interviews, podcasts, short films, event documentation, and community stories. Creators retain rights; licenses are clear and revocable where appropriate.

Creator Studio

Low-cost or sponsored recording, video, editing, photography, and publishing support, with priority for emerging creators and people without reliable equipment or production space.

Live New Haven

Regular poetry, music, film, and variety programs in accessible neighborhood venues, including creator pay, public documentation, and pathways for first-time performers.

Media skills lab

Workshops and mentorship in audio, video, copyright, distribution, budgeting, and sustainable creative practice for youth and adults.

Community media partnerships

Co-produced projects with schools, libraries, neighborhood groups, cultural organizations, colleges, and public agencies—without turning the platform into government propaganda.

Local discovery infrastructure

Editorially independent playlists, guides, profiles, and updates that help residents find local work and help creators reach audiences beyond their immediate circles.

3. Proposed legal and operating structure

The current business should not simply relabel itself “nonprofit.” A nonprofit corporation and federal tax exemption are separate steps, and the public benefit must be protected by governance, records, and operations.

LayerProposalWhy it matters
Legal entityNew Connecticut nonstock corporation with charitable purposes, dissolution language, and no private ownership interest.Creates a durable public-serving entity rather than a personal project.
Federal statusApply to the IRS for 501(c)(3) recognition using Form 1023 or, only if eligible, Form 1023-EZ.Allows the organization to represent its exemption to funders and issue compliant donation receipts.
GovernanceFive to nine directors, a majority independent of the founder and any vendor; committees for finance, programs, and governance.Shows that the mission is accountable to the community and not operated for private benefit.
Existing companyInventory the LLC’s equipment, code, domain names, contracts, debt, content rights, and liabilities before any transfer or license.Protects donors, creators, the public, and both entities from conflicts or improper value transfer.
RevenueBlend grants, donations, sponsorships, memberships, ticket income, training fees, and carefully reviewed earned revenue.Reduces dependency on a single grant and supports long-term operations.

Minimum governance package

Before fundraising at scale, adopt bylaws; a conflict-of-interest and related-party transaction policy; whistleblower, document-retention, gift-acceptance, safeguarding, accessibility, and data/privacy policies; an annual budget; approval controls; creator agreements; and a public annual report. The board should approve any contract involving a founder, director, family member, or affiliated company after disclosure and disinterested review.

Political independence

AHOY can educate the public, document civic life, and host fair, nonpartisan forums. A 501(c)(3) organization cannot support or oppose candidates for public office. Any civic programming should use consistent access rules, factual editorial standards, and written separation between the charity’s work and any individual’s political activity.

4. The practical transition roadmap

  1. Decide what is charitable.Write the public-benefit theory, define the first three programs, and identify which activities are commercial, experimental, or outside the mission.
  2. Build the founding board.Recruit New Haven voices across arts, youth services, finance, law, technology, fundraising, and neighborhood leadership. Document recusals and independent judgment.
  3. Complete legal and financial due diligence.Have a Connecticut nonprofit attorney and nonprofit accountant review the existing LLC, ownership, contracts, taxes, liabilities, intellectual property, compensation, and proposed licenses or transfers.
  4. Incorporate and prepare the operating system.File the nonstock corporation, obtain an EIN, open a dedicated bank account, approve bylaws and policies, establish bookkeeping, and create a board calendar and records system.
  5. Use a fiscal sponsor if timing requires it.A qualified sponsor can receive restricted charitable funds and support a pilot while the new organization is being formed. The sponsor agreement must define fees, control, reporting, and ownership of work.
  6. Apply for federal and Connecticut recognition.Submit the complete IRS application with a realistic three-year budget, program narrative, compensation plan, conflict disclosures, fundraising plan, and governing documents. Register or claim the appropriate exemption for charitable solicitation in Connecticut.
  7. Launch a measured pilot.Run one archive project, one creator-training cycle, and a small series of public events. Publish participation, creator-pay, audience, accessibility, and financial results before expanding.
  8. Build the grant calendar.Map city, state, foundation, university, library, and national arts opportunities to the organization’s actual programs, eligibility, match requirements, and reporting capacity.

5. What funders and public partners will need to see

Evidence of need

Listening sessions, creator surveys, neighborhood input, and a baseline of who currently has access to production, distribution, events, and training.

Evidence of delivery

A track record of AHOY events, published work, creator relationships, audience reach, and lessons learned—separated from future promises.

Evidence of equity

Transparent selection, fair creator compensation, accessible venues, language access where appropriate, and reporting by neighborhood, age, and other relevant measures.

Evidence of stewardship

Independent board oversight, clean books, restricted-fund tracking, insurance, rights documentation, safeguarding, and an annual public report.

New Haven’s current Neighborhood Cultural Vitality Grant materials emphasize arts and cultural engagement, social cohesion, youth training, equitable partnerships, and projects that advance the city’s Cultural Equity Plan. AHOY should design its pilot around those public outcomes while applying only when the entity and project meet the published requirements.

6. The civic ask

We invite New Haven’s elected officials, cultural staff, philanthropic partners, libraries, schools, universities, artists, and neighborhood organizations to help test whether this public-benefit model fills a real need.

Specifically, AHOY is seeking:

  • Listening meetings with creators and community organizations in multiple neighborhoods.
  • Introductions to nonprofit legal, accounting, governance, and fiscal-sponsorship advisors.
  • Letters of collaboration or pilot-site interest from qualified community partners.
  • Guidance on public-space access, youth programming, accessibility, and city grant eligibility.
  • Early philanthropic support for legal formation, community research, creator pay, and a first-year pilot.

The immediate question is not whether AHOY deserves a grant. It is whether New Haven wants to help build accountable, local media infrastructure that returns cultural value to the people who make and live here.

Proposed first-year measures

Publish 50 local works; pay at least 30 creators; provide six training sessions; host 12 public programs; reach 500 residents in person; document free public access; and publish a year-end report with program results, creator compensation, demographics collected ethically, and every major funding source.

Sources and important note

This proposal is a planning document, not legal or tax advice. Before incorporating, transferring assets, soliciting donations, paying insiders, or representing AHOY as tax-exempt, obtain advice from a Connecticut nonprofit attorney and nonprofit accountant.
